# Reading: documents and question types

All documents are written fresh for the session. Invented facts stay plausible; no real person is quoted, and
invented figures are not attributed to real organisations.

## Exercise 1: reading to choose (lire pour s'orienter)

**Situation:** a person or group has a need and four or five requirements. Four short documents (ads, offers,
programme descriptions, about 60 to 80 words each) each meet some requirements and miss others.

How to build it:
1. Write the situation in two or three sentences, with 4 or 5 requirements: budget, dates, place, a
   must-have, a must-not. Example of a need: a company outing for twelve people, a language course, a flat to
   share, a gym membership, a weekend away with a child and a dog.
2. Write four options. Exactly one meets every requirement. Each of the others fails on one or two, and the
   failure is stated indirectly (« fermé le lundi » when Monday is needed, « à partir de 15 personnes », a price per
   person that has to be multiplied).
3. Task: a grid with the requirements as rows and the options as columns, tick « convient » or « ne convient pas »
   for each cell, then name the option to choose.

Tests: scanning, comparing, small calculations, reading conditions and exceptions. Id: `comprehension/ce-reperage`.

## Exercises 2 and 3: reading to understand (lire pour s'informer)

**Document:** an article of 350 to 450 words in a general-press style: a title, a short lead, four or five
paragraphs. It reports a trend or an initiative, quotes one or two people (invented, first name and role), and
contains both facts and opinions, with at least one reservation or opposing view.

Calibrating to B1: frequent vocabulary with five to eight less common words whose meaning can be worked out from
context; sentences of 15 to 25 words; passé composé, imparfait, futur, some conditionnel and one or two subjunctives.
Exercise 3's text is a little longer and more argumentative than exercise 2's.

**Questions (8 to 10 per article, in the order of the text):**

| Type | What it tests | Build it so that |
|---|---|---|
| Multiple choice, general | topic, the writer's aim, the tone | wrong options are too narrow, too broad, or true but secondary |
| Multiple choice, detail | a fact, a reason, a consequence | the right option rewords; a wrong one reuses the text's words with a changed detail |
| Vrai / faux + justification | a precise statement checked against one passage | the statement rewords the passage; « faux » items change one element (who, when, how many, negation) |
| Who thinks what | attributing an opinion to the right person | two people hold views that sound close but differ |
| Meaning in context | a word or expression, explained or matched to a synonym | the context gives enough to decide |
| Short open answer | a reason or an example the text gives | answerable in under ten words taken or adapted from the text |

Mix per article: 1–2 general, 3–4 vrai/faux with justification, 2–3 detail, 1 opinion, 1 meaning in context.

The exam itself uses closed questions. The justification and the short open answers are training devices: they
show whether the right answer came from the right passage. Ids when missed: `comprehension/ce-justification`
(right V/F but wrong or missing proof, answers too vague), `comprehension/ce-reperage` (looked in the wrong place).
